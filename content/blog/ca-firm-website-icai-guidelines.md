Most business websites are built to persuade: client logos, glowing reviews, a pricing table, a "trusted by 500+ businesses" banner. A chartered accountant's website can't use any of those. ICAI's Code of Ethics restricts how members advertise, and it has specific guidelines for what a CA firm's website may and may not show.

That catches a lot of firms out, because most web designers don't know the rules either. A site that looks perfectly normal to a designer can put a CA in breach of the Code. This guide covers what the guidelines rule out, what they allow, and how to build a site that still brings in enquiries within those limits.

*This is a practical summary, not legal advice. ICAI revises its guidance from time to time, so check the current [website guidelines advisory on icai.org](https://www.icai.org/post/advisory-website-guidelines-of-the-institute) before you publish.*

## What a CA firm's website can't show

Under ICAI's website guidelines and Code of Ethics, these are off the table:

- **Fees.** No fee amounts, no "starting at" prices, no packages with prices — and no "free consultation" either. ICAI has clarified that the quantum of fees, including free of charge, shouldn't be mentioned.
- **Client names and logos.** No client list, no logo wall, no "we work with" section — for past or present clients.
- **Testimonials and endorsements.** No client quotes, star ratings or review widgets. Embedding your Google reviews on the site would in effect be a testimonials section, so leave it off.
- **Self-praise.** Superlatives like "leading", "best" or "top-rated" read as laudatory advertising. Describe what you do, not how good you are at it.

ICAI has also advised members against listing on third-party aggregator platforms, partly because those sites typically show client names and fees. That makes your own website more important, not less: it's the main place you control how the firm is presented.

## What a CA firm's website can include

The guidelines leave plenty of room for a useful, professional site. Built around factual information, a CA website can include:

- **Firm particulars** — firm name, year of establishment, office address, phone, email, and office hours.
- **Partners and team** — names, qualifications, memberships and areas of practice, stated factually.
- **Services** — a plain description of each area you handle: audit and assurance, direct and indirect tax, GST registration and returns, company incorporation, ROC compliance, accounting and bookkeeping. Describe the work, not the price.
- **Articles and updates** — explanations of GST changes, filing reminders, compliance calendars. This is where a CA site can genuinely stand out, and it gives clients a reason to return between filing periods.
- **Careers** — openings for articleship and staff.
- **Contact and appointments** — an enquiry form, an appointment request, a map and directions.

The tone throughout should be informative rather than promotional. Done well, that suits the profession anyway: a clean, factual site signals the same care clients expect from their accountant.

## How a compliant CA website still brings in clients

Without reviews or prices to lean on, a CA website wins enquiries in three other ways.

**It's easy to find.** Most people looking for a CA search something like "CA in Gurugram for GST" or "chartered accountant near me". A site with a clear page for each service, your locality named naturally, and fast mobile loading has a real chance of showing up for those searches. A one-page site with a phone number usually doesn't.

**It answers questions before the call.** Someone choosing a CA wants to know whether you handle their kind of work. A services page that explains what's involved in GST registration, or what a small company needs for ROC filings, does that job — and it's exactly the kind of educational content the guidelines allow.

**It makes contact simple.** An appointment request form, a WhatsApp link and a clear phone number matter most from January to March, when ITR and advance-tax work peaks and people want an answer quickly. A secure way for clients to upload documents also saves a lot of back-and-forth over email during filing season.

## Getting the build right

When you brief a web designer, tell them up front that the site has to follow ICAI's guidelines, and check the first draft against the list above. The usual slips are a pricing section copied from a template, a "Clients" page, or a reviews widget dropped into the footer.

On timing and budget, a CA site is a straightforward build: typically 5 to 10 pages with a contact form and appointment booking. ## Frequently asked questions

**Can a CA firm show its fees on its website?**
No. ICAI's guidelines say the fees charged shouldn't be mentioned, and that includes describing a service as free. Discuss fees with each prospective client directly.

**Can I put client logos or testimonials on my CA website?**
No. Client names (past or present), client logos and testimonials are all ruled out by ICAI's website guidelines.

**Can a CA firm website have a blog?**
Yes. Educational articles on tax, GST and compliance are allowed, and they're one of the best ways for a CA site to attract visitors from search.

**Should I list my firm on directory and aggregator sites instead?**
ICAI has advised members against listing on third-party aggregator platforms. Your own website, and ICAI's own channels, are the safer routes.

**Can my website have an appointment booking form?**
Yes. Contact details, enquiry forms and appointment requests are factual and practical, and they're among the most useful things the site can have.
