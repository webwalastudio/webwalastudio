Anyone can call themselves a "web designer" — the range runs from a student with a Canva template to a full studio with a proper process. Most bad web design experiences aren't bad luck: they come from not checking a few specific things upfront that would have surfaced the problem before any money changed hands. Here's what actually matters, and the questions to ask each candidate before you decide.

This guide assumes you're already talking to a few designers or agencies. If you're still looking for candidates, start with [how to hire a web designer](/blog/where-to-hire-web-designer).

## Start with their live work, not their pitch

Before any conversation about price, look at what they've actually built — and look at it the way your customers will.

**Relevant beats pretty.** A designer's best work might be a striking one-off for a brand very unlike yours. What matters more is whether they've built for businesses similar to yours in size and industry — a school, a clinic, a retailer — since that shows they understand the content and functionality your type of business needs, not just visual polish.

**Live sites, on your phone.** Portfolio screenshots are easy to cherry-pick or borrow. Ask for live URLs and open them on your own phone: does the site load fast, can you read the text without zooming, are the buttons easy to tap? Almost everyone claims "responsive design" now — this is the only way to check it. A portfolio full of sites from years ago, or ones that no longer load, is a sign the "recent work" isn't actually recent.

## The 6 questions to ask before you hire

### 1. "What exactly happens after I pay?"

Vague answers ("we'll get started right away!") are a warning sign. A clear answer names an actual first step — a discovery call, a content questionnaire, a kickoff form — with a timeframe attached, and can tell you how many revision rounds are included and roughly how long each stage takes. If the answer is open-ended, that's usually because there isn't a real process behind it.

### 2. "Who owns the website once it's built?"

This is the most important question that gets skipped. Some designers build on proprietary platforms you can't leave without starting over, or keep the only login to your hosting. You want a straight answer: after launch, do you fully own the code, the domain, and the hosting account? If not, every future change becomes a negotiation.

### 3. "What's the realistic timeline, and what does it depend on?"

Any agency can quote a fast number — what matters is whether they explain what that timeline assumes from you (content readiness, feedback turnaround) and what happens if those assumptions don't hold. A timeline with no dependencies named is usually a marketing number, not a plan. (We broke down what a realistic schedule looks like in [how long it should take to launch a business website](/blog/how-long-website-launch-timeline).)

### 4. "What happens after launch?"

A website isn't a one-time deliverable — bugs surface, small changes come up, and content needs updating. Ask specifically: is a maintenance period included, what does it cover, and what does support cost once it ends? The sharpest version of this question is *"What happens if I want to make a change six months after launch?"* — it surfaces the ownership and support answers in one shot. For what a maintenance period should actually cover, see [what needs updating after launch](/blog/website-maintenance-what-needs-updating).

### 5. "What's included in the price, and what isn't?"

A trustworthy quote separates the build cost from ongoing costs like domain and hosting, and states what's included — page count, revision rounds — rather than a single vague number. Extra pages, extra revisions, and hosting are the common places quotes turn out less complete than they first appeared. Get it in writing before you commit. If you're not sure what a fair quote looks like, our breakdown of [how much a website costs in India in 2026](/blog/website-cost-india-2026) gives you a baseline.

### 6. "Can I talk to a past client?"

Reviews help, but read them for specifics rather than star ratings: a review that mentions the timeline held, communication was responsive, or a bug was fixed quickly after launch tells you far more than a generic five-star comment. If you can, message a past client directly and ask whether they'd hire the same designer again.

## Freelancer, agency, or DIY builder?

- **DIY builders** (Wix, Squarespace) are the cheapest option and fine for a hobby page, but you're doing the design work yourself, and the result usually looks and performs like it.
- **Freelancers** can be excellent and affordable, but quality varies more, and there's no backup if they become unavailable mid-project.
- **A small studio or agency** typically costs more than a freelancer but less than a large agency, and gives you a defined process and more reliability than one person working alone.

None of these is universally right — the answers to the questions above matter more than the category. Our guide to [where to hire a web designer](/blog/where-to-hire-web-designer) compares each option in more detail.

## Red flags worth taking seriously

- Pressure to decide or pay immediately, without time to think it over.
- No clear answer on ownership of the finished site.
- No live work you can open and check yourself.
- Pricing that seems too good to be true relative to competitors — it usually is, in one of the ways above.
- No process at all — just "send us your info and we'll build it."

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a lower price always a red flag?**
Not always, but ask why it's lower — is it a genuinely leaner process, or is it missing mobile testing, revisions, or post-launch support that a higher quote includes? Compare price against what's included, not the number alone.

**Should I choose a designer who specializes in my industry?**
It helps, but it isn't required. What matters more is whether they understand the *functionality* your industry needs — a booking flow for a salon, an inventory-aware catalog for a retailer — even if their past clients were in a different industry.

**How many years of experience should a web designer have?**
Years matter less than a verifiable track record of finished, live, working sites. A newer studio with a handful of strong live examples is generally a safer bet than a designer with a decade of experience and no live work you can check.
